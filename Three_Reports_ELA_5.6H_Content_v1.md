# The Three Reports — Content v1 (ELA.5.6H-XP)

**Grade 5 · ELAR · Cloudreach.** Written Sept 28, 2026. Code: `lib/cases/expedition-station/quests/three-reports.js`.

**Main standard:** 5.6H · **Also shows up under:** 5.7B, 5.6G, 5.7C, 5.9D, 5.13C, 5.13E, 5.3B

**I can:** I can put together information from different sources to understand something new, and explain it in my own words.

**Mission:** Find what each report adds, spot where they agree, and put them together to explain things no single report can explain alone.

**Story:** From the floating sky reef of Cloudreach, the crew watched a hurricane grow over an Earth ocean. A scientist, a reporter, and a pilot each sent a report, and each one tells a different part of the story.

**Meters on Cloudreach:** Storm Book (starts at 20%) · Report pins · Journal (10 discoveries)

**Status:** All 3 acts are playable. Every task is graded (written answers are scored by the teacher).

## Passages

### Passage A · How a Hurricane Is Born (Science report, by Dr. Lena Ortiz, weather scientist)

A hurricane is a giant, spinning storm that forms over warm ocean water. `A1.1` It needs water that is at least 80 degrees Fahrenheit. `A1.2` That warm water is the storm's fuel. `A1.3`

First, the sun-warmed ocean heats the air just above it, and some of the water evaporates. `A2.1` This warm, moist air rises, and as it rises, it cools and forms towering clouds. `A2.2` When the water vapor turns into cloud droplets, it gives off heat, which makes the air rise even faster. `A2.3`

As the air rises, the air pressure near the ocean surface drops, and more air rushes in to take its place. `A3.1` Because Earth spins, the rushing air curves, and the whole storm begins to turn. `A3.2` When its winds reach 74 miles per hour, the storm is called a hurricane. `A3.3`

In the center of a strong hurricane is the eye, a calm area where the sky can even be clear. `A4.1` Around the eye is a wall of clouds with the fastest winds. `A4.2` A hurricane weakens when it moves over land or cool water, because it loses its fuel. `A4.3`

### Passage B · Pelican Point Rides Out Hurricane Marisol (News report, from the Pelican Point Tide)

Pelican Point, Day 6. `B1.1` By Sam Reyes, staff reporter. `B1.2`

Three days before the storm arrived, forecasters warned that Hurricane Marisol was heading toward our town. `B2.1` The mayor ordered everyone living near the beach to evacuate, which means to leave for a safer place. `B2.2` Families boarded up windows, packed water and flashlights, and drove inland, away from the coast. `B2.3`

Marisol came ashore early on Day 5 as a Category 3 hurricane. `B3.1` Its worst danger was not the wind but the storm surge, a wall of seawater pushed onto land by the storm's winds. `B3.2` The surge flooded Shell Street with water up to five feet deep. `B3.3`

Almost no one was hurt, because nearly everyone had left in time. `B4.1` "Leaving was hard, but it kept us safe," said Ana Brooks, who fishes from the town dock. `B4.2` By the next evening, Marisol had weakened to a tropical storm as it moved inland over farmland. `B4.3`

### Passage C · Into the Eye: A Pilot's Journal (Firsthand account, by Captain Joe Mendez, hurricane-hunter pilot)

Day 4. I have flown into hurricanes for eleven years, but today still made my hands sweat. `C1.1` Our job is to measure the storm from the inside, so forecasters can warn people on land. `C1.2`

As we flew toward the eyewall, rain hammered the windows, and the plane dropped and bounced. `C2.1` Then, all at once, we burst into sunshine. `C2.2` We were inside the eye, and a round wall of white clouds rose around us like a giant stadium. `C2.3`

Our crew dropped a measuring tube on a small parachute down through the storm. `C3.1` It radioed back that the winds near the eyewall were blowing 120 miles per hour. `C3.2` It also showed that the air pressure in the eye was very low, lower than on any day so far. `C3.3`

We sent our numbers to the forecasters right away. `C4.1` An hour later, they warned towns along the coast that Marisol was now a major hurricane. `C4.2` I thought about all the families who would read that warning and get to safety. `C4.3`

### Passage D · Sidebar: Sorting Hurricanes by Wind (Sidebar (from the science report))

Scientists sort hurricanes into five categories using the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale. `D1.1` The scale is based only on a hurricane's strongest steady winds. `D1.2`

Category 1: 74 to 95 miles per hour. `D2.1` Category 2: 96 to 110 miles per hour. `D2.2` Category 3: 111 to 129 miles per hour. `D2.3` Category 4: 130 to 156 miles per hour. `D2.4` Category 5: 157 miles per hour or more. `D2.5`

Hurricanes in Category 3, 4, or 5 are called major hurricanes. `D3.1` The scale does not measure rain or storm surge, which can also be very dangerous. `D3.2`

## Discoveries (Earth-true)

| Task | Discovery | Fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | One storm, three names | Hurricanes, typhoons, and cyclones are the same kind of storm. The name changes depending on which ocean the storm forms over. |
| 2 | Which way they spin | Hurricanes north of the equator spin counterclockwise. Hurricanes south of the equator spin clockwise. |
| 3 | Hurricane season | In the Atlantic Ocean, hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30. Most hurricanes happen in August, September, and October. |
| 4 | The first flight into a hurricane | In 1943, a pilot named Joseph Duckworth flew a small plane into a hurricane on a dare. He was the first person known to fly into a hurricane on purpose. |
| 5 | Who made the scale | The hurricane wind scale is named after engineer Herbert Saffir and weather scientist Robert Simpson. They made it in the early 1970s. |
| 6 | Moving water | Storm surge is often the most dangerous part of a hurricane. Just 6 inches of fast-moving water can knock a grown-up off their feet. |
| 8 | Barometers | A barometer measures air pressure. When the air pressure falls quickly, a storm is often on its way. |
| 10 | Dropsondes | The measuring tubes hurricane hunters drop are called dropsondes. As a dropsonde falls on its parachute, it sends back wind, temperature, and air pressure readings the whole way down. |
| 11 | Storm names | A storm gets a name when its winds reach 39 miles per hour. The name lists are reused every six years, but the names of very harmful hurricanes are retired for good. |
| 13 | Eyes in space | Weather satellites watch Earth's oceans from space all day and all night. They help forecasters spot new storms far out at sea. |
