## Passages

### Passage A · How Mountains Rise and Fall (Informational article)

Mountains look like they have been there forever. `A1.1` But mountains are always changing. `A1.2` They slowly rise up, and then they slowly wear down. `A1.3` Some mountain peaks poke up above the clouds. `A1.4`

Most tall mountains form when giant pieces of Earth's outer layer push against each other. `A2.1` These pieces are called plates, and they move about as fast as your fingernails grow. `A2.2` When two plates crash together, the land between them crumples and folds upward. `A2.3` The Himalayas, the tallest mountains on Earth, formed this way. `A2.4` Their name means "home of snow" in an old language called Sanskrit. `A2.5`

Some mountains are built by volcanoes instead. `A3.1` Hot, melted rock from deep inside Earth bursts out through an opening. `A3.2` The rock cools and hardens into a layer. `A3.3` Over thousands of years, the layers pile up into a tall, cone-shaped mountain. `A3.4` Mount Fuji in Japan is a volcano that people love to paint and photograph. `A3.5`

As soon as a mountain rises, wind, water, and ice start to wear it down. `A4.1` Rain washes away tiny bits of rock. `A4.2` Water seeps into cracks, freezes, and splits the rock apart. `A4.3` Over millions of years, sharp peaks can become rounded hills. `A4.4` The Appalachian Mountains in the eastern United States were once much taller than they are today. `A4.5`

### Passage B · Why Mountaintops Are Cold (Informational article)

If you climbed a tall mountain on a summer day, you might start in shorts and end in a heavy coat. `B1.1` The higher you go, the colder the air gets. `B1.2` This is true on mountains all over the world. `B1.3`

Sunlight does not warm the air very much as it passes through. `B2.1` Instead, sunlight warms the ground, and the warm ground heats the air right above it. `B2.2` High on a mountain, the air is far from the wide, warm land below. `B2.3` That is one reason the air up there stays chilly. `B2.4`

Air also cools off as it rises and spreads out. `B3.1` Near the ground, air is squeezed by all the air pressing down on it. `B3.2` Higher up, there is less air pressing down, so the air spreads out. `B3.3` When air spreads out, it gets cooler. `B3.4` At high altitudes, far above the level of the sea, the air can be freezing even when the valley below is warm. `B3.5`

On average, the air gets about 3 to 5 degrees Fahrenheit colder for every 1,000 feet you climb. `B4.1` That is why Mount Kilimanjaro in Africa can have snow and ice on top, even though it stands close to the equator. `B4.2` Its peak rises more than 19,000 feet into the sky. `B4.3`

### Passage C · Thin Air Up High (Informational article)

Air is made of tiny bits called particles that are too small to see. `C1.1` Near sea level, air particles are packed close together. `C1.2` High on a mountain, they are spread far apart. `C1.3` People call this thin air. `C1.4`

Thin air makes it harder for your body to get the oxygen it needs. `C2.1` Oxygen is a gas in the air that every part of your body uses. `C2.2` Each breath of thin air brings in less oxygen than a breath near the sea. `C2.3` At the top of Mount Everest, one breath has only about one-third as much oxygen as a breath at sea level. `C2.4`

When people climb too high too fast, they can get altitude sickness. `C3.1` They may get a headache, feel dizzy, or not want to eat. `C3.2` Some people feel very tired but cannot sleep well. `C3.3`

The best way to stay safe is to climb slowly. `C4.1` Climbers go up a little, then rest for a day or two. `C4.2` This gives their bodies time to get used to the thin air. `C4.3` Over time, their bodies make more red blood cells, which carry oxygen. `C4.4` If someone still feels sick, the surest fix is to go back down to thicker air. `C4.5`

### Passage D · Life on the Mountainside (Informational article)

High mountains are hard places to live. `D1.1` It is cold and windy, the air is thin, and food can be hard to find. `D1.2` Yet many animals have special body parts and habits that help them survive up high. `D1.3`

Mountain goats are expert climbers. `D2.1` Each hoof has a hard outer edge and a soft, rubbery pad in the middle. `D2.2` The pad grips rock like the bottom of a sneaker. `D2.3` This lets mountain goats walk along narrow ledges on steep cliffs. `D2.4` Their shaggy white coats make them look like they are wearing fluffy sweaters. `D2.5`

Pikas are small, round animals related to rabbits. `D3.1` They do not sleep through the winter. `D3.2` Instead, all summer long they gather grass and flowers and pile them under rocks. `D3.3` These haypiles become their food when snow covers the ground. `D3.4` Hikers often hear pikas before they see them. `D3.5`

Snow leopards live in the rocky mountains of Central Asia. `D4.1` Thick fur keeps them warm, and their wide, furry paws work like snowshoes. `D4.2` A long tail helps a snow leopard balance as it leaps from rock to rock. `D4.3` Its gray, spotted coat blends in with the rocks, so its prey does not see it coming. `D4.4`

### Passage E · The First Climb to the Top of the World (Informational article (history))

For many years, no one had ever stood on top of Mount Everest. `E1.1` Climbers had tried again and again, but the cold, the wind, and the thin air turned them back. `E1.2` Then, in the spring of 1953, a new team set out to try once more. `E1.3`

Climbing Everest took a huge team working together. `E2.1` The team was led by a British army officer named John Hunt. `E2.2` Hundreds of people helped, including many Sherpa guides and porters who knew the mountains well. `E2.3` Together they carried tents, food, and oxygen tanks up the mountain. `E2.4` They set up a chain of camps, each one higher than the last. `E2.5`

Two climbers, Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans, tried for the top first. `E3.1` They got within about 300 feet of the summit. `E3.2` But their oxygen gear was giving them trouble and time was running out, so they turned back. `E3.3`

Next came Edmund Hillary, a beekeeper from New Zealand, and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa mountain climber. `E4.1` Tenzing had been on several earlier trips to Everest. `E4.2` On May 29, 1953, the two men climbed a steep wall of rock and ice near the top. `E4.3` At about 11:30 in the morning, they stepped onto the highest point on Earth. `E4.4`

They stayed on top for only about 15 minutes. `E5.1` Hillary took photographs, and Tenzing left a small gift of sweets in the snow. `E5.2` Then they carefully climbed back down to their team. `E5.3` News of the climb spread around the world, and the two men became famous. `E5.4`
