## Passages

### Passage A · How Glaciers Form and Move (Science report)

Every glacier on Frostveil began as snowflakes. `A1.1` High in the mountains, more snow falls each winter than melts each summer. `A1.2` Year after year, the snow piles up deeper and deeper. `A1.3`

The weight of the new snow presses down on the old snow below. `A2.1` The fluffy flakes are squeezed into small, grainy pellets called firn. `A2.2` After many more years, the firn is pressed into solid, heavy glacier ice. `A2.3`

Once the ice is thick enough, gravity pulls it slowly downhill. `A3.1` The bottom of the glacier slides over a thin layer of meltwater. `A3.2` Deep inside, the ice bends and flows, like very thick putty. `A3.3`

Near the surface, the ice is stiff and brittle, so it cracks as the glacier moves. `A4.1` These deep cracks are called crevasses, and a thin bridge of snow can hide them from view. `A4.2`

So a glacier is not just a pile of ice. `A5.1` It is snow that has been pressed into ice over many years and set moving by gravity. `A5.2`

### Passage B · Silverrun Glacier: A Timeline (Timeline)

Frostveil Research Station has kept records of Silverrun Glacier for about 150 years. `B1.1` Here are the most important events, in time order. `B1.2`

1880: Explorers made the first map of Silverrun Glacier, and its front reached all the way to Crystal Bay. `B2.1` 1925: Station scientists hammered a row of metal stakes across the ice to see how it moved. `B2.2` 1960: The glacier's front had pulled back 2 kilometers from Crystal Bay. `B2.3` 1995: A chunk of ice as big as a town square broke off the front and crashed into the bay. `B2.4` 2020: The front was 7 kilometers from the bay, and the station built a camera tower to watch it every day. `B2.5`

### Passage C · Sidebar: How Do We Know Ice Moves? (Sidebar (from the glacier report))

You can't see a glacier move just by watching it. `C1.1` So in 1925, station scientists hammered a straight row of metal stakes across Silverrun Glacier. `C1.2`

One year later, the row was not straight anymore. `C2.1` The stakes in the middle had moved farther downhill than the stakes near the edges. `C2.2` This showed that the middle of a glacier flows faster than its sides, which rub against the rocky valley walls. `C2.3`

### Passage D · Nature's Bulldozer (Science article)

When a glacier moves, it changes the land beneath it. `D1.1` Meltwater seeps into cracks in the rock under the ice and freezes there. `D1.2` The ice grips the loose rock and plucks it away as the glacier moves on. `D1.3`

Rocks frozen into the bottom of the glacier scrape the ground like sandpaper. `D2.1` Because of this scraping, the bedrock is left with long scratches that point the way the ice traveled. `D2.2` The grinding also crushes rock into a fine powder called rock flour. `D2.3`

Over thousands of years, a glacier can turn a narrow, V-shaped river valley into a wide valley shaped like the letter U. `D3.1`

A glacier also carries the rocks it picks up. `D4.1` When the ice melts, it drops long ridges of rock, sand, and gravel called moraines. `D4.2` As a result, a moraine can show scientists exactly how far a glacier once reached. `D4.3`

### Passage E · Saving the Silverrun Ice Field (Problem-and-solution article)

Silverrun Glacier is shrinking. `E1.1` Each summer, more ice melts than new snow can replace, so the glacier's front retreats, or moves back uphill. `E1.2` If it keeps shrinking, the streams that bring fresh water to the station could run low in late summer. `E1.3`

Scientists have ranked three solutions, starting with the most important. `E2.1` The most important solution is to slow the warming of the air, because warmer air is what makes the ice melt faster than snow can replace it. `E2.2` The next most important step is careful measuring, so the station can plan ahead for its water supply. `E2.3` The least powerful solution is covering small patches of ice with giant white blankets, because blankets can protect only a tiny part of a glacier. `E2.4`

Measuring has already begun. `E3.1` Nova's team places GPS markers on the ice to track how far the front moves each year. `E3.2` The camera tower takes a photo of the glacier every day, so scientists can compare pictures from year to year. `E3.3`

On Earth, some mountain towns cover small parts of a glacier with white blankets each summer. `E4.1` The blankets reflect sunlight and can slow the melting in those spots. `E4.2`

### Passage F · Glacier or Iceberg? (Compare-and-contrast article)

Glaciers and icebergs are both made of frozen fresh water that began as snow. `F1.1` Both are made of such thick, tightly packed ice that they can look bright blue. `F1.2`

However, a glacier forms on land and moves slowly across it. `F2.1` An iceberg is a chunk of ice floating in the ocean or a lake. `F2.2`

In fact, many icebergs begin as part of a glacier. `F3.1` When a glacier reaches the sea, pieces of ice crack off its front and crash into the water, which is called calving. `F3.2` Unlike a glacier, which can last for thousands of years, an iceberg drifts into warmer water and melts much sooner. `F3.3`

## Discoveries (Earth-true)

| Task | Discovery | Fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Blue glacier ice | Thick glacier ice often looks blue. The ice is so tightly packed that it soaks up red light and lets blue light bounce back to your eyes. |
| 2 | Ice worms | Tiny ice worms live inside some glaciers in Alaska and the northwestern United States. They wiggle up to the surface at night to eat tiny algae on the snow. |
| 3 | Glacier Bay | In 1794, explorers found Glacier Bay in Alaska almost filled with ice. Since then, its glaciers have melted back about 100 kilometers. |
| 5 | Ice on Earth's land | Glaciers and ice sheets cover about one-tenth of all the land on Earth today. |
| 6 | Erratic boulders | Glaciers can carry boulders as big as houses far from where they started. When the ice melts, the boulders are left behind. Scientists call them erratics. |
| 8 | Yosemite Valley | Yosemite Valley in California was shaped by glaciers long ago. The ice helped carve its wide, U-shaped floor and steep walls. |
| 10 | Surging glaciers | Some glaciers suddenly surge. For a few months, they can race along many times faster than their normal speed. |
| 11 | Rock flour lakes | Glaciers grind rock into a fine powder called rock flour. When it floats in a lake, it can turn the water a milky blue-green. |
| 13 | The Great Lakes | The Great Lakes in North America were dug out by giant sheets of ice during the last Ice Age. Melting ice filled them with water. |
| 15 | Fjords | A fjord is a long, narrow bay with steep cliffs. Fjords form when glaciers carve deep valleys that later fill with seawater. Norway is famous for them. |
