## Passages

### Passage A · Nova's Field Guide: The Kapok Tree (Field guide page · Bold words are printed in CAPITAL letters)

Heading: A Forest Giant `A1.1`

The kapok tree grows in rainforests in Central America, South America, and West Africa. `A2.1` It is one of the tallest trees in the forest. `A2.2` Its wide top pokes out above the other trees, like a giant umbrella. `A2.3` This huge tree is a home and a food source for many rainforest animals. `A2.4`

Heading: Quick Facts `A3.1`

• Height: up to 200 feet `A4.1` • Trunk: can be 10 feet wide `A4.2` • Roots: giant BUTTRESSES that spread out from the trunk like walls `A4.3` • Seeds: wrapped in soft, fluffy fibers that float on the wind `A4.4`

Heading: Homes in the Branches `A5.1`

Many birds build nests in its high, wide branches. `A6.1` Frogs, snakes, and insects live on its bark and branches. `A6.2` Its thick branches are a safe place to rest, far above the forest floor. `A6.3`

Heading: Night Visitors `A7.1`

Kapok flowers open at night. `A8.1` They have a strong smell that bats can follow in the dark. `A8.2` Bats drink the sweet NECTAR inside the flowers. `A8.3` As they feed, the bats carry POLLEN from flower to flower, and that helps the tree make seeds. `A8.4`

Picture caption: A bat sips nectar from a kapok flower, and pollen sticks to its furry face. `A9.1`

### Passage B · Nova's Field Guide: When a Giant Falls (Field guide page · Bold words are printed in CAPITAL letters)

Heading: Crash! `B1.1`

Giant rainforest trees do not stand forever. `B2.1` A strong storm or old age can make one crash to the ground. `B2.2` Because the tree is so big, it pulls down smaller trees and vines as it falls. `B2.3` This leaves an open space in the forest called a GAP. `B2.4`

Heading: Light Changes Everything `B3.1`

Before the fall, the ground under the giant was dark and shady. `B4.1` Now sunlight pours through the gap to the forest floor. `B4.2` As a result, seeds that were waiting in the soil begin to sprout. `B4.3` Young trees that grew slowly in the shade suddenly shoot up toward the light. `B4.4` So one fallen giant makes room for a whole new part of the forest to grow. `B4.5`

Heading: Timeline of a Gap `B5.1`

• Day 1: The tree falls, and sunlight reaches the ground. `B6.1` • First few weeks: Seeds in the soil begin to sprout. `B6.2` • First year: Fast-growing plants cover the ground. `B6.3` • 5 to 10 years: Young trees grow taller than a grown-up. `B6.4` • Many years later: The tallest young tree fills the gap, and the ground is shady again. `B6.5`

### Passage C · Nova's Field Guide: Pitcher Plants (Field guide page · Bold words are printed in CAPITAL letters)

Heading: A Hungry Plant `C1.1`

Most plants get the nutrients they need from the soil. `C2.1` But some pitcher plants grow where the soil has very few nutrients. `C2.2` That is a big problem, because plants need nutrients to grow strong. `C2.3`

Heading: A Clever Trap `C3.1`

Pitcher plants solve this problem by catching insects. `C4.1` Some of their leaves grow into deep cups called PITCHERS. `C4.2` Sweet nectar on the slippery rim draws insects close. `C4.3` When an insect slips, it falls into a pool of liquid at the bottom. `C4.4` The liquid breaks the insect down, and the plant soaks up its nutrients. `C4.5`

Heading: Did You Know? `C5.1`

• Many pitcher plants grow in rainforests in Southeast Asia. `C6.1` • The biggest pitchers are more than 1 foot tall. `C6.2` • A few giant pitchers have trapped a frog or a mouse, but that is very rare. `C6.3`

### Passage D · Kai's Draft: The Okapi (Field guide draft (has a mistake) · Bold words are printed in CAPITAL letters)

Heading: Meet the Okapi `D1.1`

The okapi lives only in a rainforest in central Africa. `D2.1` It has a reddish-brown body and white stripes on its legs, like a zebra. `D2.2` But its closest relative is really the giraffe. `D2.3`

Heading: What Okapis Eat `D3.1`

An okapi's striped legs help it hide. `D4.1` In the forest, sunlight falls in thin stripes between the leaves. `D4.2` The okapi's stripes blend in with the light and shadows. `D4.3` This makes it hard for a leopard to spot one. `D4.4`

Heading: A Super Long Tongue `D5.1`

An okapi's tongue is so long that it can lick its own eyes and ears! `D6.1` It wraps its tongue around branches and pulls off the leaves. `D6.2`

### Passage E · Nova's Field Guide: Flying Frogs (Field guide page · Bold words are printed in CAPITAL letters)

Heading: A Long Way Down `E1.1`

Wallace's flying frogs live high in the rainforest trees of Southeast Asia. `E2.1` Climbing all the way down one tree and up another would take a small frog a long time. `E2.2` On the forest floor, the frog would also be easy for hungry animals to catch. `E2.3`

Heading: Built to Glide `E3.1`

These frogs have a clever way to get around: they glide from tree to tree. `E4.1` Their giant feet have skin stretched between the toes, called WEBBING. `E4.2` When a frog leaps, it spreads its toes wide, and the webbing works like a parachute. `E4.3` Flaps of skin on its arms help it glide, too. `E4.4` A flying frog can glide about 50 feet, which is longer than a school bus. `E4.5`

Picture caption: A flying frog sails between two trees with all four feet spread wide. `E5.1`
